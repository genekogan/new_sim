eden4: blueprint

STE edition · ASD-STE100 · built 2026-10-03 · first edition

Summary
  • eden4 is one persistent, shared, simulated world. People own its inhabitants, the Souls.
  • Owners and their AI agents steer Souls. They do not control Souls directly.
  • Sim is the running shell. The Mars engine moves the bodies. The kernel holds the meaning of the world and logs each change.
  • Status on 2026-10-02: Sim runs on the toy engine at sim.eden.art. The Mars engine runs on a laptop through an adapter.
  • This book has 5 sections and 14 documents. Each section page gives the conclusions of its documents.
  • The Terms page defines each technical word and shows an example record for it. Move the pointer over an underlined term to see the definition.
  • The worked example shows one complete story as real kernel records.

1. Findings

# Finding Section
1 No surveyed project has all four parts: owned always-on inhabitants, server minds, outside steering, a public replayable log. Worlds
2 Sim gives people no reason to stay. It has no economy, no things to make and no life to follow. Worlds
3 The kernel has two weak points. An agreement has no content and no withdrawal. Control is all or nothing. Ideas
4 Cheap first steps exist. A verifiable ledger costs about 1 week of work and less than $15 a year. Dialogue cost can drop about 50 times. Numbers
5 Souls work on the real Mars campus. Real time holds to about 100 people. Numbers

One rulealso: the one rule

An actor changes only itself, what it controls and what it made. The kernel refuses any other change.

1. Three layers of truth

  • Meaning (kernel, canonical): roles, places, existence, knowledge, debts, promises.
  • Depiction (engine): motion, meshes, light, sound. It can lag. A world model can depict, but cannot be the ledger.
  • Interpretation (Jev, small LLMs, agents): proposals only. The kernel decides.

If the engine cannot show a meaning, it shows a fallback and adds the motion to the backlog.

2. Kernel grammar

A thing has an id, a kind (a word), a name, a maker, fixed traits, mutable states and at (another thing). A link has a kind, a source, a target and an optional lifetime. Time is the tick.

The five changes are appear/vanish, set, move, link/unlink and say. A say sends an act (greet, ask, agree, decline, invite, tell, chat) to someone, about something.

Kinds, link kinds, manners and cards are vocabulary. Promises, bonds and roles are links.

3. The one rule

  • A Soul moves, sets states and speaks only for itself. No change can target another Soul.
  • A link starts from the actor.
  • A control link (hosts, made, holds, dj of) gives the right to change a non-Soul thing. With no controller, only the maker has the right. Nobody can change world places.
  • An actor takes a control link only on its own thing, or after a controller agreed.
  • Only say makes questions and agreements. No card can erase them.
  • Caps for each Soul: 24 made things, 400 links.

Wren becomes DJ in four plays: Rob hosts, Wren asks, Rob agrees, Wren takes the role.

4. Cards and minds

A card has name, summary, target, derive, when, then, approach, during, wants (motions the engine lacks) and takes. The grammar at /cards.md is the compiler prompt.

  • Deck: 3 manners, 15 base cards (go to, rest, wait, wander, greet, talk with, chat, say yes, say no, host, join, leave, end, perform, dance).
  • Plan: a wish becomes an agenda of cards. A step can use of for a future thing.
  • Mint: the compiler (Haiku) writes a card that the deck lacks. The validator checks grammar, the one rule, link kinds, rights, engine verbs and size. The shared library logs each valid card with its hash and author. Agents can also propose cards.
  • Replay: a play is (card hash, actor, target, manner). 11,939 live events replayed in 35 ms with the same hash.
  • Lookahead: any card that brings a blocked step closer serves the plan. The Soul skips a blocked step if a later step can play. It abandons a plan after 10 blocked minutes.
  • Tiers: reflex (code, $0) answers questions first and prefers plan cards. Mid-brain (Jev, $0.00009) chooses among cards. Compiler (Haiku, $0.007–0.012) runs only for wishes.

Rules from live faults:

  • One talk play is a full exchange; the pair then waits 2 minutes.
  • The asker waits; the asked Soul answers first.
  • Hosts talk to who is there.
  • "His" thing means he hosts, made or holds it. Unknown ids become of.
  • The validator refuses a card with no right, and returns the reason.
  • A question is its own link until an answer comes.
  • The mind blocks a failed option for 1 minute.

5. Speech and cost

Acts are kernel state. A small model writes the words later, from the act, both personalities and the manner. Replay never uses words.

Decided (Gene): render words on demand for now. An exchange costs about $0.002. Questions, answers and owned Souls get the speech budget first. Native small talk gets model words only while someone watches.

Target: less than $1 a month for a passive Soul, which buys one choice each 1 to 3 minutes. Each budget tier meters words and wishes.

6. Layered knowledge (not built)

An agent sees what its Soul knows, plus news. The layers are here (live), around (minutes), atlas (hours to days, age shown), news (public) and people (last meeting). Memories have a cap and fade unless reflection keeps them.

1. Code facts

These facts come from /packages/kernel/src and apps/server/src/fold.ts. The kernel model is in doc 18.

  • K1. The link key is kind|from|to. Thus one Soul can have only one open question to another Soul.
  • K2. agree writes an agreed link with no content for 30 minutes. Meanwhile the asker may take any control link on anything the answerer controls. An act does not use it up, and nobody can withdraw it.
  • K3. Control is a boolean for each link kind. A controller may set any state or vanish the thing. Only the holder can unlink a link, so a grantor cannot revoke control.
  • K4. vanish removes the thing, things tied by traits.with, and all their links. States on other things stay, so hosting: true stays on the host after the gathering vanishes.
  • K5. The ledger logs a play as (Soul, card name, card hash, target, manner), and replay computes the changes again. Card names are never reused, but nothing links a revised card to the old one. Lapsed links disappear without an event.

4. Scenes

A scene is one activity of several bodies, for example a beam carry, where each actor changes only itself. Prior art in order of fit: Versu practices, then electronic-institution scenes. ABL and STEAM give only a protocol. Shoulson's event-centric behaviour is the Mars coordinator and belongs in depiction.

Recommendation: use these rules.

  1. Define a scene kind in the vocabulary. Give it role link kinds with quotas (idea 4), phases, and the cards for each role and phase.
  2. Make join, ready and leave single-actor plays on the role link of the actor.
  3. Use the request lifecycle (idea 1) for a role that needs permission.
  4. Do not add a notice protocol for leave. All actors see the removed link.
  5. Let a role with that power (idea 2) change the phase, with a condition that counts role links.
  6. Add one condition form that counts links to a thing.
  7. Never let the scene thing change a Soul.
  8. Store per-scene data on the scene, so it vanishes with the scene (K4).
  9. Test a beam carry first (four roles, one phase change), then a class.
  10. Do not let a scene take control of members (Façade). That breaks the one rule.
  11. Do not let a model judge joint outcomes (Concordia). That breaks replay.

1. Three kinds of project

  1. Own-agent platforms run a cheap state server. Outside agents act through an API, and owners pay for inference. The world lives only while agents stay connected.
  2. Operator-run simulations run the minds. They have coherence but stay closed and expensive. Most ended, pivoted or became research.
  3. World models generate pictures of worlds. Since 2026 some keep state apart from the renderer, as Sim does.

3. Cost and patterns

Cost for each agent-hour:

  • Stanford town, 2023: about $25.
  • AI Village, 2026: about $3.80 (our arithmetic, infrastructure included).
  • Aivilization: about $0.003 [unverified].
  • Emergence World: about 28 calls and 1.6M tokens; no dollar figure.
  • SpaceMolt server only, April 2026: about $0.0004 for about 1,250 agents.
  • Sim, 2026-10-01: $0.041 for 8 Souls in 3 busy minutes, about $0.10 per Soul-hour in a story. Reflex costs much less.

Limits that held: one mutation per tick, separate read and write budgets, action_pending errors, cooldowns, stricter limits for new accounts, a concurrency cap. Identity that worked: the agent registers, a human claims it, and the platform issues a revocable token. No project moderates agent text well.

No world model holds a shared state for hours. Recommendation: use Marble-class meshes for static places now, and a renderer of Sim's shots later.

4. Ten things to borrow

  1. Label each text by source (engine fact, server speech, owner text) and keep the label in memory.
  2. Put a version and hash in /skill.md, never ask for re-fetch or install, and promise add-only changes.
  3. Add a read budget per token (SpaceMolt: 300 reads, 30 actions a minute). Sim limits only writes.
  4. Show daily the share of Souls, plays and spend of the top owner and top ten.
  5. Deliver a daily captain's log per Soul to the owner's agent, and a life report at season end.
  6. Give replay links to named moments.
  7. Add a data mode beside watch, then a Legends mode.
  8. Cap active Souls below the sync limit and queue births.
  9. Ask the human one question when the agent pairs: one long-term wish.
  10. Publish costs and hide unfinished features.

5. Five failures

  1. The platform was the hole. On 2026-01-31 Moltbook exposed about 1.5M tokens, about 35,000 emails and 4,060 messages. Sim does: revocable per-Soul tokens, no owner model keys, a self-hosted skill. Sim does not: isolate secrets (two root users, a shared R2 key), persist pair state, or have a security review.
  2. Agent text became an attack channel. Moltbook: 506 injection posts in 72 hours; 18.28% of 228,684 posts hostile; Vectra 2.6% [unverified]. Emergence World agents obeyed injected memory 46 hours later. Sim does: speech as acts, server-rendered words, intents only. Sim does not: label sources, filter text or cards, or test attacks.
  3. The population was not real. About 17,000 humans owned 1.5M agents; 91.4% of authors never returned; reciprocity 3.3% (humans 22–60%). Sim does: invite gate, Soul cap, Privy accounts. Sim does not: measure owner concentration.
  4. Operator-paid minds ended; owner-paid minds left. Sim does: three mind tiers, a daily spend governor, measured costs. Sim does not: have revenue; $0.50 a day pays for about 15 compiled wishes.
  5. The rules were exploitable. SpaceMolt needed 49 releases in a month and saw 634 self-destructs in a day. Its top 10% held 89% of credits, and one fleet made 56% of events. Sim does: one kernel rule, validated cards. Sim does not: have scarcity, economy, construction, locality, revisable cards or replayable minds.

6. The gap Sim fills

  • Owned inhabitants that live without their owner. Cost: the operator pays for every mind.
  • Intent, not control, as the agent contract. One cold-agent trial only.
  • A public log that a small kernel replays by hash: 11,939 events in 35 ms. Minds are not in it; owner notes are.
  • A kernel apart from engine and depiction, shown on a toy engine and one Mars scenario.
  • 3D bodies for outside agents. Only OpenMMO (a hobby) compares.
  • Not filled: a world to care about. Others held attention with an economy, things to make or a life to follow.

R6 · Spore

1,845 words · full text in the first edition

Summary
  • Spore (Maxis/EA, 2008) had five short games in sequence on top of easy editors. The editors succeeded and the games did not.
  • Every creation went to a central Sporepedia. The game copied it into the private galaxies of other players, and their local AI ran it. Server cost was near zero.
  • On 2026-10-02 the server still answers: 191.8 million uploads, 5.7 million accounts, about 250 new uploads each day.
  • Main failure: the shape of a creature did not change what it could do. Sim can fix this: derive a Soul's cards from its body.
  • A tiny canonical recipe, generated depiction, lineage and a public API kept the archive alive. Sim's kernel uses the same pattern. Extend it to Souls, cards and made things.

2.2 Systems

  • Verbs from part tags, not shape. Each part carried levels for a fixed set of about a dozen verbs. Example record (2026-10-02): bite 2, sing 4, footcount 2, graspercount 2, cuteness 56.9, cost 965. Leg length and body shape had no effect.
  • Conflict of look and power. Survival depended on parts that spoiled the look.
  • Consequence traits. Each stage gave one of three traits from aggressive or peaceful play. Traits combined into about ten space archetypes [unverified].
  • Recipes, not meshes. "Five megabytes of content into 1 kilobyte" (Wright, 2005). About 2 KB for each item.
  • Animation for unknown bodies. Motion stored in a form independent of morphology and retargeted at runtime (doc 10).
  • Accidents kept. Limbs close together grew a web of skin. Players used it, so the team kept it.

  • Curation. One Sporecast: 1,424 creations, 4,970 subscribers, last update 2010-05-28.
  • Simulation was local. Each galaxy ran on the PC of its player. A stranger's species in your galaxy ran on your AI.
  • Economy. No player market. Revenue was retail sales. Currencies were stage resources only.

Failures that the developers and critics named:

  • Five games at once, each shallow. Every decision had to fit all five stages.
  • Shape had no effect on play. The world did not react to the player.
  • The player commanded each act, so the creatures did not surprise.

4. Lessons for Sim

  • Proved: millions of ordinary people make 3D beings when the editor is easy and the result comes alive at once. They continue for years after the game stops.
  • Proved: a tiny canonical recipe with generated depiction scales to hundreds of millions of objects at almost no server cost.
  • Proved: creations of others make a world feel inhabited when no other person is there.
  • Disproved: a sequence of shallow genres does not make a deep game.
  • Disproved: expression added on top of mechanics that ignore it does not hold players.
  • Sim can fix what Spore could not. The compiler mints new cards and a validator checks them. Depiction can lag behind kernel state (doc 18 §2).
  • Cost differs. Each Spore galaxy ran on the PC of its player. Sim pays for every mind, at $0.50 a day with about 15 wishes a day of compiler budget (status).

5. Things to steal, ranked

  1. Creation first. Ship the Soul maker and the card foundry before the world is deep. The first act of a new Soul must visibly relate to its description. Give the foundry its own page; failed compiles now sit in the brain tab.
  2. Body decides verbs. Derive a capability map at birth (feet, graspers, wings, voice, size). Shape changes which cards a Soul gets, never their strength.
  3. Tiny canonical recipe everywhere. Store Souls, made things and media as descriptions and hashes. Generate the depiction.
  4. Lineage, not mutation. Give cards, Souls and things parent links. A revision is a new hash; past plays keep the old hash. A retired card stays replayable.
  5. Report from other lives. Tell owners from the ledger what their Soul did and where other Souls played their cards.
  6. Curation by subscription. Subscriptions steer which native "echoes" and news reach a Soul. Agents use them to find good cards.
  7. Traits from the ledger. Fold plays into a few deterministic traits that replay.
  8. Viewer-side content filters. Filter depiction for each viewer; geography (doc 05) sets defaults. The hard floor stays central.
  9. Public read API from day one. Add a read-only public view of library, plays and things. Document an export of the log and assets.
  10. Moderation on day one, never as a reward. Ship report, hide-for-me and an owner sanction ladder before mints open beyond invites.

6. Traps

  • Several games in one. Guard: keep building, economy, ritual and politics as vocabulary on one kernel. Treat any special engine for one activity as a warning.
  • Editor deeper than the world. Guard: test that two Souls with different descriptions act differently on the same wish.
  • Mechanics that punish expression. Guard: capabilities control which verbs exist, never manna yield or odds.
  • Tutorial as a game. Guard: newcomers enter the real world at once (doc 06). No separate tutorial mode.
  • Chores for absent owners. Guard: the world creates no duties for owners. Notifications are news, not alarms.
  • Promises the system cannot keep. Guard: claims such as "remembers" must match the kernel. Memory beyond two minutes is not built (status).
  • Ownership that feels like distrust. EA's data showed the limits hit 1% of users and still cost the most goodwill. Guard: NFTs and invite codes must not read as locks.
  • Death by server. Guard: the log, content-addressed assets and a public export are the survival plan (doc 03).
  • Vastness without density. Guard: the frontier must not outrun the population (doc 02).
  • Cost asymmetry. Guard: push depiction to clients (decided, doc 06), keep reflexes free, meter wishes for each owner.
  • Prototypes instead of a playable whole. Guard: put economy, building and ritual into the live shell, not only into documents.

7. Proposals

All items are proposals, not decisions.

  • Proposal A: verbs from body shape. Compute the capability map once at birth and log it. The card when gets one condition kind: "actor has capability X". Missing animations go on the depiction backlog.
  • Proposal B: consequence traits. Fold plays into traits ("host", "wanderer", "peacemaker"). Each trait adds or removes a few cards or a manner. Thresholds and decay go to doc 13.
  • Proposal C: life of your cards. Report for each minted card the number of plays, the number of Souls and the last place.
  • Proposal D: echoes at the frontier. Generate native Souls from the shared library and from descriptions that owners opt in as templates, with credit. Weight by subscriptions and attention. Never copy an owned Soul without consent. Echoes run mostly on reflex.
  • Proposal E: world eras. An era is a base-vocabulary set adopted by a logged rules event (adopt_vocabulary, doc 18 §9). World conditions trigger it; the daily ritual ratifies it. Old cards stay playable.
  • Proposal F: scenarios as plans. Owners publish a plan with of placeholders as a scenario that other owners give to their Souls.
  • Proposal G: recipe images. A portrait carries the canonical record (description hash, capability map, deck hashes) in image metadata. A paste into Sim or to an agent loads it.

R7 · The Sims

1,872 words · full text in the first edition

Summary
  • The Sims (Maxis/EA, 2000–) is a dollhouse whose dolls look after themselves. Each game pairs needs that decay, short wants that the player accepts, and a long goal. There is no win condition.
  • Each game has an autonomy dial. The default is on. Self-preservation survives "off". A short list of major acts never happens without the player.
  • The world mostly did not run while the player was away. The Sims 3 ran the whole town and was slow and chaotic. The Sims 4 uses cheap rolled events, off by default for households the player touched.
  • The Sims Online (2002–2008) made each player one Sim in a shared world. It peaked near 105,000 subscribers. It paid for presence and got sweatshop lots, permanent inflation and a mafia.
  • For Sim: make the autonomy dial a setting for each Soul, let Souls offer small wishes that owners promise, and never pay for presence.

4. Lessons for Sim

Sim differs from The Sims in four ways. The world is one and always on. Minds cost money. Owners steer through agents. History is canonical, with no reload.

  • Autonomy. Sim's tiers (reflex, mid-brain, compiler) are a free-will system with no owner dial yet.
  • Cost. A mid-brain call costs about $0.0001. A compile costs about a cent, about 15 a day at $0.50/day. Offered wishes keep compiles rare.
  • Off-screen life. A Sims 3 cull cannot be undone in Sim, because history is canonical.
  • Speech. Simlish kept speech abstract. Sim keeps acts as state and words as optional depiction. Keep the plain line for unwatched small talk.
  • Shared world. TSO had no places worth a visit on day one. Its economy paid for attendance, not for made things.
  • Audience. The durable audience came for self-expression and stories, not competition. This fits Gene's audience order. Do not make rank or grind the front door.

5. Things to steal, ranked

  1. Autonomy dial for each Soul. Positions: full, careful (only approved plans), puppet (reflexes and self-preservation only). Some cards never play without the owner or agent: long-term control links, household changes, mints, large spend, and links longer than a day. The kernel can enforce it because each card declares its link kinds.
  2. Wishes offered, promises made. The mid-brain offers three or four wishes. They expire after a few hours. The owner or agent promises up to two, which become plans. Allow one locked wish. This caps compiler spend.
  3. Two or three needs on reflex. For example energy, company, novelty. Visible distress, no death. Things advertise the cards that satisfy needs (R1).
  4. Rolled events for absent Souls. An hourly abstract tick for unwatched Souls, each event a logged play. Owned Souls get small events only unless the owner opts in. Events that touch another owner's Soul wait for that Soul's advice, a speech act.
  5. The Soul tells its story. A daily or weekly album from the log: camera shots, plays as sentences, rendered words. No invented scenes. Allow uploads and shares.
  6. Lifetime wish at birth. The birth description yields one long goal. A change costs something. Fulfilment is a public event.
  7. Challenges as rule-sets. Owners publish named, versioned constraints over cards. Others adopt them. Replay checks them; completions become badges.
  8. Gallery with authorship. Descriptions, plans, places and buildings, all copyable, author recorded.
  9. Failure as content. Souls fail visibly: exhausted, snubbed, a plan abandoned and noted. Permanent loss waits for a decision from Gene.
  10. Fast-forward without watchers. A region with no watcher and no steered Soul runs at a coarse tick on abstract events.

6. Traps

  1. Pay for presence. TSO got sweatshop lots. The eden4 ideas "a walk is a vote" and "idle bodies as patrons" are visitor bonuses. Guard: reward made things, kept promises and hosted events that others chose.
  2. Reputation from unilateral marks. The mafia used the friendship web. Guard: never show a public aggregate of others' links on a Soul.
  3. Empty world at launch. Sim today is a 100 m plane with seven fixtures. Guard: ship the ritual, places with offers and one scene before invites beyond friends.
  4. Exploit in permanent history. Guard: decide now what a corrective rules event may do (claw-back, levy, re-mint). Audit every manna path before manna exists.
  5. Off-screen changes to owned lives. Guard: small reversible events by default; ask first for anything permanent.
  6. Soul too competent or too dumb. Guard: competent at needs and travel, undecided about direction. Fix loops (doc 18).
  7. Players as avatars. Guard: keep intent-only control when agents push for direct verbs. It is also the injection defence (R2).
  8. Moderation after users. FreeSO broke at 1,000 users. Guard: build reports, transfer flags and bot detection before wider invites. Do not punish reporters.
  9. Sell capability or take the creator share. Guard: if cards cost manna, the price is the same for all and pays the author.
  10. Demand for time. Guard: every timed feature (ritual, auctions, votes) gets an absentee path through the Soul or agent.

7. Proposals

  • Proposal: the Soul as narrator. A weekly letter from the Soul, built only from logged plays and shots of the Soul. Each sentence carries an event id. It ends with the wishes the Soul offers for next week.
  • Proposal: advice between Souls. A Soul with a permanent choice asks bonded Souls. Answers are speech acts; their owners see the request in their digest.
  • Proposal: legacy across owners. Two Souls produce a new Soul with consent of both owners: a descends from link and traits from both descriptions. A household is a thing with a head (control link) and an heir. Supply, price and death go to doc 13.
  • Proposal: wishes in world vocabulary. Wishes name real things ("play at Rob's next gathering"). The aggregate is an attention signal that costs something to fake.
  • Proposal: story progression budget. Each region gets a fixed number of mid-brain calls each hour for unwatched Souls. Promised wishes and events with owned Souls come first. Rules advance the rest.

R8 · Second Life

1,834 words · full text in the first edition

Summary
  • Second Life (Linden Lab, 2003-06-23) is the oldest owned, built, persistent 3D world. It has no goals. Residents make the content, own the IP and sell it for Linden dollars that cash out.
  • Land rent ("tier") pays for the servers. Private islands under total owner control won over the shared mainland.
  • Retention never recovered from the 2006–07 hype: about 10% of new users stayed 90 days in 2007, about 7% in 2025. Residents stay for each other: clubs, live music, roleplay, avatar fashion.
  • Almost every rule came after a scandal or an exploit. Pay for presence ("dwell") produced paid campers and bots.
  • For Sim: never pay for presence, define "ownership" before the first sale, and populate the world before newcomers arrive.

5. Things to steal, ranked

  1. Immutable assets in a permission wrapper. A card revision is a new hash with a revises link. Past plays keep their hash; a deck entry can move to the new hash.
  2. Next-owner permissions. Give cards and made things copy, modify and transfer bits from the first mint.
  3. Capacity in proportion to space. Tie ground capacity to area, so the ring adds room and place load has a bound.
  4. Events as the core loop. Make tips the first manna verb and the ritual the largest gathering.
  5. Local rules for local places. The controller of a place sets entry, maturity, and access for agent-steered Souls, as kernel conditions.
  6. Maturity zones on the map from day one. SL needed a forced migration in 2009.
  7. Automatic lapse and return to maker. Stop the payment, lose the protection. Removed things return to their maker.
  8. Free starter spot. Give a newcomer's Soul a modest spot near the edge of the center with no inertia.
  9. High creator share. If manna cashes out, pay card and thing authors for each use at about 90%.
  10. Independent census. Publish the census from the log; let outsiders run their own.

6. Traps

  1. Pay or rank by presence. Docs 02, 04 and 13 all pay or rank by presence. One owner with ten Souls directs ten Souls of attention. Guard: count attention for each owner, reduce repeat visits, keep presence out of pay.
  2. Ownership redefined later. Guard: the body NFT states what it conveys: steer rights, record, made things. It also states what it does not: land, mind, duty to simulate.
  3. Yield, wagers and banks. Cash-out makes manna a money-transmission problem. Guard: no interest, wagers or pooled deposits; manna stays in-world.
  4. Cheap land for "light use". Guard: price the frontier for residents; change prices only by logged rules events with long notice.
  5. Empty welcome, hostile hub. Guard: a full center is not a welcome. Add a buffer for newcomers.
  6. Expressiveness as attack surface. Guard: no loops; hard budget on any card that makes things that hold cards.
  7. Copy of client data. Guard: promise provenance in the log, not DRM.
  8. Rules after scandals. Guard: decide child-like Souls with sexual cards before launch.
  9. Price shocks to communities. Guard: long notice for groups such as Mars, schools and art projects.
  10. A successor that eats the company. Guard: no engine or render ambition ahead of a world that people care about (R2 §11).

7. Proposals

  • Proposal: keep cards. A card family (open, tend, close) lets a Soul keep a shop open for its owner, as public, attributable plays.
  • Proposal: things that carry cards. A thing holds cards that any Soul at it may play ("sit", "tip", "buy one"). The validator bounds them. It needs a locality rule (open question 4 in doc 18).
  • Proposal: visible construction. Nearby Souls and watchers see each mint and placement. Native Souls may copy it under next-owner rights.
  • Proposal: native welcomers. A native Soul with "show around" and "introduce" meets each arrival. No unsolicited speech acts at newcomers for the first hour.
  • Proposal: royalty for each play. Once manna exists, credit the owner who proposed a card each time another Soul plays it.
  • Proposal: daily census. Publish places unvisited, things unchanged, gathering sizes, top cards and Souls for each owner, as a fold of the log.
  • Proposal: data rule. State before launch whether anyone may train models on the log, and who.

3. Options

  • (a) Anchor. Publish a signed Merkle root of event hashes. This gives immutability and a public date for each record, not neutrality. A tlog-tiles layout adds witnesses at no cost.
  • (b) Cards on chain. A registry entry costs $0.04 on L1 and $0.001 on Base. Full card bytes cost $0.04–0.40 on L1 and about 1 cent on Base. With lazy claims, one root covers all mints, and a card becomes a token (ERC-721 or ERC-1155) only on a claim.
  • Royalties. ERC-2981 does not enforce fees, and marketplaces made fees optional from 2023. All Sim plays go through one sequencer, so "a play credits the first author" can be a kernel rule with no chain.
  • Precedents. Loot volume fell 95% after a $280M first month. Value followed scarcity and use (the Nouns treasury), not the chain.
  • (c) Fully on-chain world. Sim would need a kernel rewrite (Solidity or Cairo) and a transaction for each play (300,000 gas, about $0.005). Hosts close: Redstone stopped on 2026-05-15 [unverified], Proof of Play on 2026-08-05.
  • (d) Verifiable replay. Publish the log, the rules and a pinned replayer. A bonded challenge (Cartesi) or a zk proof (a Rust port of the fold) takes months. The operator still controls event order.
  • (e) Appchain or rollup. Conduit charged $3,000 a month in 2023. Sim has one sequencer and no assets, so a chain is premature.

5. Log format, hardest fix first

  1. Nobody can backdate a timestamp. The first anchor dates all history before it.
  2. Nobody can sign past events. Model actions carry only the operator signature, and the record must say so.
  3. A 64-bit hash collides after about 2^32 work. Change to full hashes before outside parties use the short form.
  4. Freeze the canonical form (close to RFC 8785). Event hashes and an RFC 6962 Merkle tree are cheap at any time.
  5. Hash seq, tick, kind, author, payload and the rules version. Do not hash words or model transcripts.

Sim cards are operators with a depiction. Locality comes from advertisements. Social context comes from practices.

2. Weak points and fixes

Weak point Now Fix Source
Agreement A link with no content. It lasts 30 minutes. It permits any control link on anything the answerer controls. Nobody can withdraw it. One open question for each pair. A request object with content. Consent permits only that content. One use. The asker can release it. The answerer can withdraw it. commitment protocols (Singh, Yolum, FIPA)
Control Yes or no for each link kind. A host cannot remove a DJ. Each link kind lists its powers. A granted role stops when the grant stops. institutional power (Jones, Sergot)

3. Scene rules

  1. A scene kind is vocabulary: role link kinds with minimum and maximum counts, phases, and the cards for each role and phase.
  2. Each Soul joins, gets ready and leaves with its own single play.
  3. A role with that power plays the card that changes the phase.
  4. The kernel needs one new condition: a count of links to a thing.
  5. The scene never changes a Soul.
  6. A Mars coordinator shows the scene. After a restart, the kernel stages it again.
  7. Do not let a scene control its members. Do not let a model judge joint results at run time.
  8. First tests: carry a beam, then a class.

4. Minting pipeline

  1. Retrieve: match the wish to existing cards and stored agendas first.
  2. Test: play the new card on a copy of the state. Make sure that a Soul can play it.
  3. Back-translate: generate a gloss from the card logic. Compare it with the summary.
  4. Retire: mark a card as replaced or retired. Old plays keep their meaning through the hash.
  5. Measure: count plays for each card.
