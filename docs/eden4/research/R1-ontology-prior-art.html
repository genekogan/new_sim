Eden4 · Sim · research R1

Ontology prior art: a guided tour

Fourteen systems that already tried to describe a living world as structured state plus named operators, read against the Sim kernel (things, links, five changes, cards, one rule).

Our kernel, on one screen

The reference for every comparison below. Source: eden4/18-KERNEL-CARDS-AND-SELF-CHANGE.md §3–5.

Thesis

Almost every part of the kernel has a tested ancestor. Cards are STRIPS operators with a depiction attached; decks are GOAP action sets and the card library is Voyager's skill library; “acts are state” is Singh's public semantics for speech acts; the one rule is Versu's design rule that practices only suggest and each character decides. The prior art is ahead of us in three places: agreements that carry content and a full life (Yolum and Singh), powers that are specific and revocable rather than all or nothing (institutional power), and a first-class way to hold several actors in one activity over time (Versu's practices). The tour runs from formal planning to social simulation to LLM worlds and ends with those three as the ranked ideas and a recommendation for scenes.

1 · STRIPS, PDDL and GOAP

1971 → 1998 → F.E.A.R., 2005

What it is

STRIPS (Fikes and Nilsson, 1971) describes an action as preconditions, an add list and a delete list, and assumes anything not listed stays the same; a planner searches for a sequence of actions that reaches a goal. PDDL standardised the notation for planners; version 2.1 added durative actions and numbers, and 2.2 added derived predicates (facts computed from other facts) and facts that switch on at a set time. Jeff Orkin's goal-oriented action planning (GOAP) brought this to games in F.E.A.R.: each character has a set of actions with preconditions and effects and plans a short sequence towards its current goal, so the character's state machine shrinks to three states. Hierarchical task network (HTN) planners such as SHOP2 instead store “methods” that decompose a task into subtasks until only primitive operators remain.

Maps to our kernel as

A card is a STRIPS operator. when is the precondition list, then the effects, target and derive the typed parameters. set and move replace a single value, so they need no delete list; a link's lifetime is a timed fact. A Soul's deck is a GOAP action set, and doc 18's “looking one step ahead” is a search of depth one.

What to take / what to beware

Take: because cards are already operators, standard planning applies cheaply: a reachability check at minting (“can this card ever be played?”) and lookahead of several steps without a model. An owner's agenda is an HTN decomposition done by the compiler; storing the decompositions as minted methods would let a cheap matcher reuse them. Derived predicates would remove duplicated state: base cards set hosting: true and also create a hosts link, and only one card clears the state. Beware: PDDL can say things our ten condition forms cannot (conditional or “for everyone” effects, numbers, conditions that must hold throughout). And F.E.A.R. kept one description of effects for the planner and another for the game; Evans and Short name that gap as why agents retry actions that cannot succeed. Sim is safe while the card that is evaluated is the card that is applied.

Primary sources: Orkin, “Three States and a Plan: The A.I. of F.E.A.R.” (GDC 2006) · Fox and Long, PDDL 2.1 (JAIR 2003) · Nau et al., SHOP2 (JAIR 2003) · STRIPS overview

2 · The Sims: smart objects and advertisements

Maxis, 2000

What it is

In The Sims the objects carry the behaviour. Each object's interaction has a procedure that does it, a check of whether it is possible, and advertisements saying which needs it satisfies (hunger, energy, fun, social and so on). A Sim not under the player's control picks, from all behaviours of all objects, the one that most raises its current happiness; advertisements fade with distance and can vary with personality. The chosen procedure then runs in the Sim's own thread. When two Sims interact, an invisible social-interaction object is created and both Sims' threads pass into it. New content is a new object: no Sim has to change. Free will was on by default and the player could override it, but some acts never happened autonomously: looking for a job, asking someone to move in, proposing, WooHoo (R7 §1).

Maps to our kernel as

An advertisement is a card offered by a Thing instead of held in a Soul's deck; needs are states on the Soul; the selection is a reflex-tier scorer. The invisible social-interaction object is the smallest possible scene Thing: something that exists only while two actors do one thing together.

What to take / what to beware

Take: cards offered by things in reach as the default locality rule (doc 18 open problem 4): a Soul may play its own cards and those offered where it is. It also gives made things a purpose (open problem 3) and lets user-made objects extend everyone's action space. Beware: the object's procedure drives the Sim, which our one rule forbids; the Soul must remain the actor of every change. And a Sim judges an action by what it advertises, not what it does: Forbus and Wright suggest “skeptical Sims” that compare before and after, and Evans and Short cite the Sim that retries a blocked toilet for ever. Doc 18 met the same loop and patched it with a one-minute cooldown. The companion study R7 adds a lesson from play: a per-Soul autonomy dial with a short list of cards that never play without the owner.

See also: R7 — The Sims: gameplay, players, and what a shared Sims taught (how people played, The Sims Online, ten things to steal).

Primary source: Forbus and Wright, “Some notes on programming objects in The Sims” (2001) · later: Ingebretson and Rebuschatis, “Concurrent Interactions in The Sims 4” (GDC 2014)

3 · Dwarf Fortress

Bay 12 Games, 2006 →

What it is

Dwarf Fortress generates a whole world and then simulates its civilisations (settlements, trade routes, wars) before the player arrives, so play starts inside a history. Tarn Adams's published principles are about method: do not overplan the model; break a system into its basic elements and let their interplay produce the result (biomes come from separate temperature, rainfall and drainage fields); “operate at the level of what the player sees or one layer below”; base the model on real-world analogues. Each dwarf carries dated memories with the feeling and its cause, as in the screenshot above. No author description of the social model was found; third-party reverse engineering (DFHack) shows joint activities stored as records, each a tree of events with a participant list.

Maps to our kernel as

Things with small traits and states, a log of events, and stories read off afterwards: the case for our approach. The ledger read as history is DF's Legends mode. A dwarf's memory list is what doc 18 open problem 5 lacks: a remembered event with a date, a feeling and a cause. A DF activity record is a scene Thing with member links and sub-events.

What to take / what to beware

Take: “one layer below what the player sees” as the test for what belongs in the kernel; derive outcomes from a few underlying facts rather than storing them (the biome lesson, and the cure for duplicated state); a legends view over the ledger, which needs no kernel change. Beware: every DF kind, and every activity type, is hand-written code; it is the closed-vocabulary route, rich only where someone wrote the code. Emergent absurdity is charming in a single-player game and a support problem in a shared world with owners.

Primary source: Adams, “Simulation Principles from Dwarf Fortress”, Game AI Pro 2, ch. 41 (2015) · third-party: DFHack activity structures

4 · Façade and ABL

Mateas & Stern, 2005

What it is

Façade is a one-act interactive drama with two characters, Trip and Grace, and a player who types freely. Content is organised in beats, the smallest unit of dramatic action; each beat has beat goals, and handlers that wake when the player does something, interrupt the current goal and respond. Typed text is interpreted as a discourse act (for example, a reference to Grace), and reactions are authored against acts, not words. The characters are written in ABL, a reactive planning language; its central addition is the joint behaviour: a behaviour shared by a team, entered and exited together through a three-phase commit (one member announces its intention, the others reply, all signal ready), with each member performing its own steps.

Maps to our kernel as

Discourse acts are our say acts: the same split between act and words, run in the opposite direction (they parse words into acts; we render acts into words). The joint-behaviour commit is ask → agree → act turned into a reusable protocol for entering and leaving a shared activity. A beat is a scene-sized operator with preconditions and effects on story state.

What to take / what to beware

Take: the idea that entering a shared activity is itself negotiated, which is ask → agree. Exit needs less in Sim: a member who leaves removes its own role link, everyone can see that, and they re-plan, so no notice protocol is required. Take handlers too: reactions that interrupt, answer and return to what was under way. Beware: the authors say the assumption of strong autonomy “is problematic” for story characters, and design against it: one author writes both minds, and a joint behaviour holds both characters. Our one rule is strong autonomy. Evans and Short add that joining depends on having a matching behaviour, not on wanting to. And the cost: they count Façade's 27 beats against more than a thousand Versu actions.

Primary source: Mateas and Stern, “A Behavior Language for Story-Based Believable Agents” (2002)

5 · Comme il Faut, Prom Week and Ensemble

UC Santa Cruz, 2010–2015

What it is

Comme il Faut (CiF) is a “social physics” engine built at UC Santa Cruz and used in Prom Week. Social state is traits, statuses, relationships and numeric networks between characters, plus a social facts database holding the history of what happened. A social exchange is one character's attempt to change the social state between itself and another (start dating, become allies). Weighted influence rules decide what each initiator wants and with whom (volition); the responder accepts or rejects by its own rules; an instantiation then performs the outcome with effects and templated dialogue; trigger rules fire afterwards to keep definitions true (dating two people makes a “two-timer”). Prom Week used more than 5,000 rules. Ensemble repackaged CiF as a general engine whose schema declares, for each kind of social fact, whether it is boolean or numeric, directed or reciprocal, its default and a duration after which it reverts.

Maps to our kernel as

Relationships, networks and statuses are Links (Ensemble's duration is our lifetime); the social facts database is our ledger; one exchange bundles our ask, answer and effect; volition rules are what our mid-brain does with a small model, written as data instead; Ensemble's schema is the link-kind table we keep in code and prose.

What to take / what to beware

Take: declare direction, reciprocity, default and lifetime per link kind; allow conditions over history (“did X in the last n ticks”, “ever”), which the ledger already holds; consider derived consequences in the spirit of trigger rules, so cards need not repeat definitions. The responder deciding by its own rules is our one rule exactly. Beware: the rule count, and debugging thousands of weights. Evans and Short's criticism also applies to us: an exchange is an isolated act, with nothing to give a sequence of acts continuity. A third party can be referenced in conditions and effects, but CiF has no group activity. That is the scenes gap.

Primary sources: McCoy et al., “Social Story Worlds With Comme il Faut” (IEEE TCIAIG, 2014) · Ensemble schema · Prom Week

6 · Versu: social practices and roles

Evans & Short, 2013–2014

What it is

Versu is an engine for text-based interactive stories (the official site lists genres from comedy to romance to thriller) in which every character is an autonomous agent. Coordination comes from social practices: descriptions of recurring situations (a greeting, a meal, a game of whist) with roles and a state of their own. In the authors' words, practices “never control the agents directly; they merely provide suggestions.” Several practices run at once, and a character's options are the union of what they offer; an agent judges an option by applying it, scoring the resulting state against its desires and undoing it. Norm violations are permitted, and recorded so that others can react. The world is a set of sentences in exclusion logic, where a.b says b is one of the ways a is and a!b that it is the only way.

Maps to our kernel as

The closest ancestor overall. A practice instance is a Thing with role links; “an affordance exists only inside its practice” is a card whose when requires a link to that Thing (as dance requires attends). The ! operator is the single-valuedness of set and move. And the authors' design rule is ours: “Relationship state changes should require active choice from both characters.”

What to take / what to beware

Take: scenes as practices (ranked idea 3); uniqueness declared once on a link kind and type-checked, where we repeat noneLinkedTo in each card; breaches as recorded facts others can respond to, where our kernel can only forbid; the inspector that answers “why did this precondition fail?”. Beware: the authors report more than 300 desires whose tuning was “a difficult and unrewarding problem”; their mechanism for letting one practice suppress the others was “too broad and crude”; all agents share one understanding of each practice's state; and a twenty-minute episode took one to two months to write until a higher-level authoring tool was built.

Primary source: Evans and Short, “Versu—A Simulationist Storytelling System” (IEEE TCIAIG, 2014) · reconstruction: Praxish (AIIDE 2023)

7 · Talk of the Town and Kismet

Ryan et al., 2015 · Summerville and Samuel, 2020–2021

No real image: the Talk of the Town repository README and the papers' landing pages carry no linkable screenshot or figure, and Kismet's original 2020 paper was not located. The diagram below is ours, drawn from the 2015 paper's description of belief facets.

What it is

Talk of the Town simulates a small town over decades before play; its characters, in the authors' words, “observe and form knowledge about the world, propagate knowledge to other characters, misremember and forget knowledge, and lie.” Each character holds a mental model per person or place it knows, made of belief facets, one per attribute (workplace, hair colour, whereabouts). A facet records its value, whether it is accurate, the facet it replaced, and its evidence; its strength is the sum of that evidence, and each piece records source, location, time and a strength that decays. A lie is received as a statement. Kismet is a small social-simulation language with near-English rules (“if Drinker is drunk and Drinker and Target don't like each other”); its authors' follow-up reports that this phrasing “enhances readability, but it hurts writability”.

Maps to our kernel as

Doc 18 §6 (“Knowing”) and open problem 5 (memory): a belief is a link from a Soul to a Thing whose data holds value, evidence and strength, and our said is a statement event. City of Gangsters (section 10's sources) does the same for relationships: a relationship's value is the sum of dated, expiring, explained history entries.

What to take / what to beware

Take: explained, dated evidence on social links (ranked idea 9): knowledge with provenance can be wrong, stale, second-hand or a lie, which answers doc 18's open question of saying something new (it becomes the hearer's belief, not world truth), and lets the interface say why. From Kismet: a near-natural vocabulary breeds synonyms (is “doesn't like” the same as “dislikes”?), so keep card JSON canonical and generate a plain-language rendering for Gene rather than a second syntax. Beware: per-character belief stores grow fast, and our bound is 400 links per Soul; decide what is forgotten before what is kept.

Primary sources: Ryan et al., “Toward Characters Who Observe, Tell, Misremember, and Lie” (AIIDE 2015) · Summerville et al., “Casual Creator Cursed Problems” (2021) · code: github.com/james-owen-ryan/talktown

8 · Speech acts, FIPA-ACL and social commitments

Austin 1962 · Searle 1975 · KQML 1994 · FIPA 2002 · Singh 1998 · Yolum and Singh 2002

What it is

Saying is doing: Austin separated what is said from the act performed in saying it, and Searle's 1975 classification gives five kinds of act: assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, and declarations, which change reality by being said (naming, appointing). KQML and then FIPA-ACL turned this into message types (“performatives”) for software agents, with protocols such as the request protocol above. FIPA defined each act by the speaker's beliefs and intentions. Singh (1998) argued that private mental states cannot be checked among independent agents, that “communication is inherently public”, and that acts should be defined by the public commitments they create. Yolum and Singh then define a commitment as a public fact, a debtor owing a creditor an outcome, with operations and who may perform each; a protocol becomes any sequence of acts that resolves the commitments. Institutional-power work (Jones and Sergot) adds that an act can count as creating an institutional fact; electronic institutions build agent societies from role-based scenes with quotas and entry and exit states.

Maps to our kernel as

say is (act, about), and “acts are state, words are depiction” is Singh's public semantics, already in place. An open asked link is a pending directive; an agreed link is a commitment from answerer to asker; control: true on a link kind is one hard-coded counts-as rule, and “take the role only if a controller agreed” is empowerment.

What to take / what to beware

Take: give requests and agreements identity, content and the full lifecycle (ranked idea 1): consent should permit only what was asked, be used up when acted on, and be releasable by the asker and withdrawable by the answerer; and replace boolean control with specific, revocable powers (idea 2). Add the two act classes our base acts lack: declarations, and commissives with content (a promise to do X), which is doc 18's open question about saying something new. Beware: the companion analysis found this the weakest point in the kernel: agreed has no content, lets the asker take any control link on anything the answerer controls for thirty minutes, and cannot be withdrawn; and links keyed by kind, sender and receiver allow only one open question per pair. Never let a rule depend on what a Soul's model “believes”.

Primary sources: Singh, “Agent Communication Languages: Rethinking the Principles” (1998) · Yolum and Singh, “Flexible Protocol Specification and Execution” (2002) · FIPA Request Interaction Protocol · Esteva et al., AMELI (2004)

9 · Inform 7 rulebooks

Graham Nelson, 2006 →

What it is

Inform 7 is Graham Nelson's language for interactive fiction, written in English-like sentences. The world is kinds (room, thing, container, person…) with properties, and relations whose shape is declared once (“Loving relates various people to one person”). Every command becomes an action that passes through rulebooks in a fixed order: before, instead, check, carry out, after, report. Check, carry out and report belong to the action; before, instead and after are shared rulebooks into which rules about any place or object can be written. Inform also has scenes: spans of time with cues to start and end.

Maps to our kernel as

Kinds, properties and relations are Things, states and Links; containment is at. Check is when, carry out is then, report is the depiction and the rendered words: the same separation of meaning from telling. What we lack is the left-hand half: no way for a place, host or object to modify how a general card works in its domain.

What to take / what to beware

Take: declared relation shape (one-to-one, various-to-one) as a property of each link kind, replacing repeated noneLinkedTo conditions; house rules later: the controller of a place may attach rules that intercept plays there, which answers locality with local law. Beware: Inform is single-player and turn-based, with one actor at a time and no consent; an instead rule written by someone else is exactly acting on another, so only a thing's controller should attach rules to it. Interacting rulebooks also become hard to predict; Nelson himself calls the benefits of natural-language code “double-edged”.

Primary sources: Writing with Inform §13.5 (relations) · §10.1 (scenes) · Inform 7 Handbook: action processing

10 · Entity-component-system vs facts with time

Game engines (Bilas, 2002) · RDF · Datomic, 2012 · Dedalus, 2009 · event sourcing

What it is

In an entity-component system an entity is an id, components are plain data attached to it, and systems are code run over all entities with given components; it stores the current state, laid out for speed. City of Gangsters' authors relate their social model to this tradition (Bilas, 2002), which puts every game object behind a general query interface. Fact stores keep time instead. RDF stores subject–predicate–object triples. Datomic stores immutable facts [entity, attribute, value, transaction, added?]: a retraction is another fact and the database can be read as of any past transaction; its schema guidance is to grow and never break, never remove or reuse names, and annotate superseded ones. Dedalus adds an explicit time argument to Datalog, so persistence and change are rules. Event sourcing keeps an append-only log as the truth and separates a command (a request that may be refused) from an event (what happened).

Maps to our kernel as

A Thing is an entity with two open bags of data (traits and states); a Link is a triple plus value, data and lifetime. The ledger is a transaction log, but of plays, which are commands: their changes are recomputed on replay, so replay is correct only under the code that first ran them (doc 18 relies on the code hash), and lapsed links vanish without a logged event. derive and the link conditions are a small fixed-shape Datalog query.

What to take / what to beware

Take: log each play's resulting changes as well (ranked idea 3), making replay depend only on the five-change applier and turning lapses into events; this also gives provenance (“why is Wren the DJ?”) and as-of views for memory and a legends view. From Datomic's schema rules, a library lifecycle: names are already never reused, but nothing records which card supersedes which (idea 7). Derived predicates as named rules (idea 8). When a relation needs identity, content or several instances, promote it to a node: requests and agreements have reached that point. Beware: history grows without bound and needs snapshots; ECS “systems” are global code that changes state with no actor, which our one rule rules out except for a few declared world rules such as expiry.

Primary sources: Datomic data model · Datomic best practices · Alvaro et al., Dedalus (2009) · Zubek et al., City of Gangsters (2021)

11 · Generative Agents (Smallville), then AI Town

Park et al., 2023 · a16z and Convex, 2023

What it is

Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) put 25 characters driven by a language model in a small sandbox town. Each keeps a memory stream of observations in natural language, retrieves memories by recency, importance and relevance, periodically reflects (writes higher-level conclusions back into the stream) and plans its day. The world's response is also decided by the model at run time: the authors prompt it to “ask what happens to the state of the object”. One agent was seeded with the intention to throw a Valentine's Day party; invitations spread and five agents came. AI Town is an open-source descendant on a real-time engine backed by a database: agents call models outside the engine and may change the world only by submitting typed inputs. Its one joint activity, the conversation, has members who are invited, walking over, or participating.

Maps to our kernel as

Generative Agents is the opposite allocation to ours: state is free text and the model decides each effect, so nothing can be validated, owned or replayed. AI Town draws the same line as doc 18: models propose, the engine disposes. Its conversation is a small scene with consent, and “walking over” is a state we lack: agreed, but not yet there.

What to take / what to beware

Take: memory retrieval and reflection for open problem 5, applied over kernel facts and rendered words rather than replacing them; AI Town's membership states for scenes. Beware: the paper reports agents breaking norms that live only in prose, such as several entering a one-person bathroom or shops after closing; the lesson for us is to put a norm in state the kernel checks. And in AI Town the conversation is hand-coded: an engine of typed inputs still needs a general way to define joint activities.

Primary sources: Park et al., “Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior” (UIST 2023) · AI Town ARCHITECTURE.md

12 · Voyager's skill library, and Project Sid / PIANO

Wang et al., 2023 · Altera, 2024

What it is

Voyager (Wang et al., 2023) plays Minecraft with GPT-4. An automatic curriculum proposes the next task; the model writes JavaScript for it, runs it, reads the game's feedback and errors, and retries; a separate critic call judges whether the task succeeded. Only then is the program added to an ever-growing skill library, indexed by the embedding of its description, from which the five most relevant skills are retrieved for each new task. Project Sid (Altera, 2024) ran 10 to more than 1,000 agents in Minecraft with PIANO, an architecture of concurrent modules whose outputs pass through one “cognitive controller” so that speech and action agree; agents also voted on amendments to a tax constitution.

Maps to our kernel as

The skill library is the card library and adding a verified skill is minting; a deck is per-agent retrieval. The difference is the language: Voyager's skills are arbitrary code, our cards a small language over five changes. Sid's controller exists because an agent may answer “Sure thing!” while its function call does something else; Sim renders words from the act, so that cannot happen.

What to take / what to beware

Take: retrieve before minting (embed the wish, fetch the nearest cards, compile only if none fits: open problem 6); play a new card on a copy of the world before keeping it; back-translate a card into plain language and compare it with its summary. Beware: Voyager's authors report the critic missing successes, invented game facts and calls to functions that do not exist, and in its code a skill with an existing name silently replaces the old index entry. Later work (Berlot-Attwell et al., 2024) found that in two library-learning systems “function reuse is extremely infrequent”: expect most minted cards to be played once, and measure it. Sid's results come from one company's report; no independent replication was checked.

Primary sources: Wang et al., “Voyager” (2023) · Altera, “Project Sid” (2024) · Berlot-Attwell et al., “Library Learning Doesn't” (2024)

13 · Concordia: the game-master pattern

Google DeepMind, 2023 →

No real image: the repository README carries only badges, the 2023 paper has no HTML version on arXiv with figures, and neither the 2025 follow-up's HTML nor DeepMind's publication page offers a figure that could be linked. The diagram below is ours.

What it is

Concordia is DeepMind's library for generative agent-based models, built on the tabletop role-playing pattern. Agents state what they want to do in natural language; a Game Master model “decides on the outcome of their attempt” and generates event statements. The paper lists its duties: keep a consistent, grounded world state, tell agents what they can observe, decide the effect of actions, and resolve what happens when actions submitted by several agents conflict. Chosen experimental quantities, such as the money an agent holds, are “grounded variables” that the Game Master records and checks; in physical settings it checks plausibility.

Maps to our kernel as

Grounded variables are their kernel; everything else is judged at run time. Concordia's “attempt in words, then a resolved event” is our wish, card and play, with the judgement compiled once into a card instead of made at every step.

What to take / what to beware

Take: conflict resolution is someone's stated job; Sim should name where simultaneous plays on the same thing are ordered. A model in the Game Master role is useful at minting time, as a plausibility check on a new card. Beware: a model referee is not deterministic, cannot be replayed, costs a call per action, and can be steered by the text it reads. It must never be the ledger, and it must not judge joint outcomes in scenes at run time.

Primary source: Vezhnevets et al., “Generative agent-based modeling with actions grounded in physical, social, or digital space using Concordia” (2023) · code: github.com/google-deepmind/concordia

14 · Infinite Craft

Neal Agarwal, 2024

What it is

A browser game that starts from four elements (water, fire, wind, earth) and has one operation: combine two named elements into a new one. According to press reports summarised on Wikipedia, the game first checks its database for the pair; if the pair is new, a Llama model names the result and picks an emoji, and the result is saved so that the same pair always gives the same answer for every player. The first player to make an element sees it labelled “First Discovery”. A content filter screens results, and some are still incoherent.

Maps to our kernel as

Doc 18 §1 names it as the model for “unlimited named things, one fixed operation”. The stored recipe table is our card library: mint once, then replay for ever without a model. First Discovery is the author recorded at minting.

What to take / what to beware

Take: key the library by the question as well as the answer, so that a repeated wish finds its card instead of minting a near-duplicate (part of ranked idea 7); credit minters visibly; apply mint-once to more than cards (kinds, link kinds, depictions). Beware: a poor result is permanent once stored, so new cards need quarantine or a way to be superseded; and Infinite Craft's elements are names with no effects, so there is nothing to validate. It shows the open half of our design and none of the structured half.

Source: the game · Wikipedia summary of press reports (no primary statement by the author on the mechanism was found)

Ranked: the most actionable ideas

Ranked by expected value for the kernel as it stands, from the companion analysis (R1-ONTOLOGY-PRIOR-ART.md §6), which checked the kernel code. Its code facts, cited in the table:

  • K1. Links are keyed by kind, sender and receiver, so one Soul can have only one open question to another.
  • K2. agreed has no content and lasts 30 minutes; while it stands the asker may take any control link on anything the answerer controls; it is not used up and cannot be withdrawn.
  • K3. Control is all or nothing, and a grantor cannot remove a grantee's control link.
  • K4. vanish removes a thing's links, but states on other things stay: hosting: true outlives the gathering unless end the gathering was played.
  • K5. Plays are logged as commands and recomputed on replay; names are never reused, but a revised card has no link to the old one; lapsed links leave no logged event.

Recommendation, from the companion analysis

Define the meaning of a scene as a Versu-style practice built from kernel parts, and keep doc 19's proposal (one coordinator stages a scene Thing and is re-staged from the kernel) as its depiction.

  • A scene kind is vocabulary: its role link kinds with minimum and maximum counts (ranked idea 4), its phases, and which cards each role may play in each phase.
  • Joining, readying and leaving are single-actor plays that write or remove the actor's own role link. A role that needs permission uses the request lifecycle (idea 1). Leaving needs no notice protocol: the removed link is visible to everyone, and they re-plan.
  • Phase changes are cards played by a role with that power (idea 2), conditioned on the role links (“at least four carriers are ready”). This needs one new condition form that counts links to a Thing.
  • The scene Thing never changes a Soul. The coordinator runs while quotas are met. When the scene vanishes, its role links go with it; per-scene data should be stored on the scene so it dies too.
  • First test: carrying a beam (tight coupling, four roles, one phase change), then a class (asymmetric roles).

Avoid scenes that take control of members (Façade's joint behaviours, drama managers), which break the one rule, and a model judging joint outcomes at run time (Concordia), which breaks replay. Other prior art worth a look for the protocol: electronic-institution scenes (role quotas, access and exit states per role), AI Town's membership states, and event-centric behaviour (Shoulson et al.), where one structure drives several bodies, as a Mars coordinator already does in depiction.
